I haven't posted here in just over a year. I'd love to tell you I was off having adventures, but the truth is I've been elbow deep in stuff.

The house needed doing, and the house is winning. Anyone who has done any sort of DIY to an old house knows the arc. You start on Saturday morning full of enthusiasm, and by Sunday you're on first-name terms with the damp in the corner, wondering what else has been quietly rotting behind the mortar while nobody was looking. Come to think about it, DIY is merely debugging with more fresh air and worse tooling.

There's been a little photography too - not as much as I'd like, but some.

Mostly, though, it's been work. Work has been hard. Not hard in the fun way. Hard in the way that eats your evenings and leaves you staring at the wall by Thursday.

There used to be a social contract in security. You found a vulnerability, you told the maintainer privately, you gave them time to fix it (ninety days was the convention[^1]) and then you published together: patch out, advisory out, everyone moves on. Like most things that are good, it was never the law. It was a norm, held together by reputation and basic decency, and it worked because everyone understood the point of the exercise.

Much like most social contracts, that contract is dying and AI is killing it from both ends. LLMs are writing vulnerable code at industrial scale, so there's more to find. And automated scanners are mass-producing the finding itself, filing reports as public issues within hours of discovery. No private channel. No grace period. A bot doesn't have a reputation to protect. A scraper has never internalised the ninety-day convention. "It" doesn't wait, because waiting is something humans agreed to and nobody asked it.

Worse, the reports are often wrong. Hallucinated attack paths, "critical" findings that require the attacker to already be root. Maintainers, mostly volunteers, now have to pan an unending sludge of machine-generated false positives for the real nuggets buried in it.

Noise is winning.

Which is why WireGuard continues to come in clutch. WireGuard is the [nano](/2024/01/praise-of-nano/) of VPNs (jk). It's 4,000 lines of code, does one job, and comes with no ceremony.[^2] It refuses to be interesting, which is precisely what you want from the component standing between the open internet and everything you run.

I've come to realise that I can't win the patch race. I doubt anybody self-hosting on their evenings can. What I can do is make sure almost nothing of mine is reachable in the first place. Services sit behind WireGuard. The only thing listening on the public internet is one boring UDP port. The carousel spins on, and most of it can't touch me. Boring technology is now a security strategy. Sadly for those of us who actually enjoy this, it might be the only one that works.

One genuinely good thing that came out of the day job this year was [hl7types](https://github.com/KeironO/hl7types). For the blissfully unaware, HL7v2 is the messaging standard that has shuffled patient admissions, test results, and pharmacy orders between hospital systems since the late 1980s. In its standard form it's pipe-delimited, `MSH|^~\&` and all that, and it is the actual, literal plumbing of the NHS and most hospitals on earth. If you work in health informatics you cannot escape it. Believe me, I've tried.

The Python tooling for it, though, is subpar. That's the polite version. I got fed up enough at work that I did the thing you're not supposed to do and built my own.

hl7types gives you typed, Pydantic-powered models for every HL7 v2 message, segment, and datatype from v2.1 through v2.8.2, generated straight from the specification, with ER7 and XML serialisation built in. A message stops being a string you squint at and becomes a structure your editor understands, validates, and autocompletes.

The genuinely damning part is that it was easier than it should have been. The spec is public. The model is regular. Pydantic does the heavy lifting. The models themselves are generated by a second little tool I wrote. This should have existed a decade ago, and the fact that the default experience for handling clinical data says a lot about how much healthcare software values its own plumbing.

Python remains, [infuriatingly](/2024/02/fuck-you-python/), the right tool for this. If you want spec-generated models, you want the ecosystem with Pydantic in it. It's MIT-licensed, on PyPI, and the docs live at [hl7types.readthedocs.io](https://hl7types.readthedocs.io/en/latest/).

That's the update so far. DIY, patches, and a library. I'd usually call that a post and stop there.

It increasingly feels like everything is being set up to kill off the glimmer in the eyes of anyone who still glimmers. You know the glimmer. It's the thing some people still have when they talk about the work, the light that says they give a shit, that the thing they're making matters to them beyond the paypacket.

You can spot it in meetings, usually in the person who still thinks a broken system is something worth fixing. Then you watch what happens to them.

The reward for caring is more work, because caring is read as capacity. You're the one who gets handed the extra project or asked to spend Saturday fixing something that should have been dealt with months ago. And when you suggest dealing with the underlying problem, rather than applying another sticking plaster, someone invariably treats your enthusiasm as evidence that you haven't been around long enough to know better.

The people without it float upwards, unburdened. I keep seeing the same thing happen to people who genuinely care about their work. They take on more, get worn down, and eventually stop volunteering to fix things. The system carries on much as it did before, except there's one fewer person willing to argue with it. And now it doesn't even need to replace them with another pair of eyes. It can replace them with something that never had any.

The saddest part of the AI discourse for me is the emotional toll, and almost nobody talks about it. Grief is embarrassing, and engineers (who are sadly overrepresented by emotionally stunted men) are trained and told to pretend they don't do feelings.

Spend your entire life getting good at something. I started learning this stuff as a teenager, spent years studying it, and have spent the better part of two decades reading, practising, making mistakes and trying to get better at doing it properly. You accumulate judgement along the way. You learn which shortcuts are going to come back and bite you, which problems are worth solving, and when the clever solution is going to be a maintenance nightmare for somebody else.

Then you watch a discourse emerge whose central joy seems to be telling you that none of it counted.

Craftspeople have always had to absorb new tools. I've spent most of my working life doing exactly that, usually with some enthusiasm. But there's a particular strand of the AI discourse that treats the years spent learning a craft as wasted effort. The caring was the waste. The devotion was the bit we're deleting.

The thing you spent your life getting good at is probably going, or going to become unrecognisable, and the discourse isn't particularly sombre about it. Some of the people loudest about vibecoding talk like they've gotten away with something. There's a peculiar glee in being able to produce something without having to understand very much about how it works, and an impatience with anyone who thinks understanding still matters.

I find that harder to stomach than the technology itself. I've no particular desire to spend the rest of my life defending every tool and technique I learned twenty years ago. Plenty of them deserved to die. But I do resent the suggestion that all the years spent acquiring judgement were some sort of embarrassing detour.

I have my own beliefs about the viability of all this. They're sceptical, they haven't changed, and they're almost beside the point. Even if the plateau arrives tomorrow, the message has already been delivered and received: caring about craft was a bad investment. You can be right about the technology and still lose the room.

There's a scene I keep thinking about. Data, the android who, by definition, does not make mistakes, loses a strategy game to a smug alien grandmaster and concludes that he must be malfunctioning. He goes to Captain Picard and asks to be relieved of duty, because in his arithmetic, losing is proof of defect. Picard's answer:

> It is possible to commit no mistakes and still lose. That is not a weakness; that is life.

I've carried that line since I was a teenager and always filed it as wisdom about grace in defeat, true in principle, the way proverbs are true. It's more real than I had previously thought. It might be the realest thing I know right now.

Here's the irony. I've spent years writing about rigged games. The entire point of everything I write about politics is that the system is structured, that effort and merit are not fairly converted into outcomes, that good people lose all the time because the table is tilted. I believed all of it. I just never believed the rigging extended to my particular table. Somewhere underneath the analysis I was still holding the craftsman's faith: do the work well, care about it, and the work will carry you.

That wasn't a strategy. It was just faith. The last two years have been a long lesson in its limits.

Which brings me to the job.

It's five years since I joined the NHS, and I've slowly fallen out of love with it. Slowly, the way you don't notice the water heating until you're dreading a place you used to believe in.

I want to be honest about the ledger, because the temptation with a post like this is to flatten everything into misery, and that would be false. I've made real progress in loads of areas that I am genuinely incredibly proud of. There are systems and processes that run better, tooling that gets used, and things that work because I turned up and made them work. I can point at them. They're real, and I'm grateful for them and for the opportunities I've been given.

But the areas that matter to me personally, the reasons I went into health data in the first place, the things I've literally written [manifestos](/2024/02/free-ehr-manifesto/) about, I have ultimately failed on.

The open, interoperable, humane way of doing health data that I thought I'd spend five years helping to build is more or less where I found it. And God knows I've tried.

I learned the systems, built the trust, shipped improvements, and played the long game exactly as you're supposed to play it. I spent years believing that if I could demonstrate the value of doing things properly, the argument would eventually make itself. Sometimes I thought I was getting somewhere. There were meetings where people agreed, projects that looked promising, and moments when it felt like the direction might finally be changing.

And then another year passed.

The structure didn't move, because structures don't move for correctness, and five years of doing it right is just five years of doing it right.

Truth be told, I feel like an abject failure.

I know that isn't the objective truth. The objective truth is the ledger above. But the feeling doesn't consult the ledger.

The feeling is Data's: I lost, therefore something in me is defective. And the answer to the feeling is Picard's: it is possible to commit no mistakes and still lose.

Last October I wrote that the Labour Party was gone, decisively, probably irreversibly, and that something was genuinely liberating about finally admitting it.

A year on, Andy Burnham has come in, and Labour looks, improbably, saved. The polls have recovered. People talk about the party as a live option again rather than a cautionary tale. I've been [wrong](/2025/10/being-wrong/) on this blog before, recently, publicly, and happily, and I'd genuinely love to be wrong again. I'd go as far as to say that I'm wrong more often than not, and if you want to gauge how things will go, ask me and the opposite will almost certainly happen.

But I'm apprehensive, and the apprehension is the promise itself. Burnham has vowed to end forty years of neoliberalism. That is a remarkable vow from a man who spent the middle stretch of those forty years as a minister in the governments that built the thing. He was Chief Secretary to the Treasury during New Labour. He ran the Department of Health at the height of the PFI era, while the NHS was mortgaging hospitals it couldn't afford to build any other way.

When he says he'll end the era, part of me wants to believe him. I also remember who was in government when much of it was happening, and I wonder how much of the promise will survive contact with the machinery of government.

I wrote a whole post about why the left needs [movements, not messiahs](/2025/10/on-heroes/), and I meant it. So the sight of a party being "saved" by the arrival of one man makes me uneasy. We've been here before, with an enormous amount of hope invested in one person and very little thought given to what happens when they're gone. I'd like to see something being built underneath Burnham that could survive him.

I'll judge it the way I judge everything: by whether life in places like the Rhondda materially changes. Rents, wages, waiting lists, buses and trains that actually turn up, children not going to bed hungry, the end of a genocide.

The damp behind the mortar ultimately doesn't give two fucks about a press release or a TikTok video.

I've spent most of this year learning the difference between maintaining things and fixing them. The house got the way it did because problems got painted over rather than dealt with, and dealing with them has meant stripping back to the brick in places. Sometimes you find something worse underneath, which is unpleasant and expensive, but at least you know what you're dealing with.

I've seen the same tendency in software and at work. We keep things running because they need to run, and eventually the workarounds become so familiar that nobody remembers why they're there. You can spend years maintaining something that ought to have been rebuilt.

Parties, I suspect, are much the same. Which is the charitable read of Burnham: maybe he really is prepared to strip back to the brick rather than repaint. I hope so. But I'll believe the forty years are ending when I see the mortar off.

Ultimately, I'm not going to win.

The discourse isn't going to turn around and apologise to the craftspeople. The structure at work isn't going to move because I've suffered at it convincingly enough. The party isn't going to be rebuilt by my hoping at it. I've had to come to terms with how little control I have over things that matter enormously to me, and I'm still struggling with that.

The glimmer is smaller than it was five years ago, and I'd be lying if I said otherwise. I check on it sometimes the way you'd check a pilot light.

It's still lit, though. I've been away from this blog for a year, and somehow the things that brought me back were the same things I've spent most of that year feeling exhausted by. The craft, the health data, the Valleys, the state of our politics and the society that directs it. I still care about all of them, despite having acquired quite a lot of evidence that caring is an excellent way to make yourself miserable.

Caring is the one malfunction nobody has quite managed to patch out of me.

I used to think that if I cared enough, worked hard enough, and got enough things right, the outcomes would eventually follow. I don't believe that anymore. I'm not sure quite what replaces it, or how you go about keeping the pilot light going when you're no longer convinced that the work will be rewarded.

But I keep coming back to Picard.

It is possible to commit no mistakes and still lose. That is not a weakness. That is life.

[^1]: The ninety-day convention is most closely associated with Google's Project Zero, whose policy is to publish findings after ninety days whether or not a patch has shipped. Even that hard-deadline version of the norm assumed a human on each end of the transaction.

[^2]: Jason Donenfeld's original pitch for WireGuard was that you could audit the whole codebase in an afternoon: roughly 4,000 lines, against the hundred-odd thousand of OpenVPN plus OpenSSL. Fewer lines, fewer bugs, less to trust.
